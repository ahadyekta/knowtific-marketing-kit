## 1. Creators vs influencers

- **Creator**: up-and-coming, roughly 0 to a couple of hundred thousand followers, no big celebrity. Value: **they make good content for you.** Follower count matters less.
- **Influencer**: established; when they say something, their audience acts. Value: **their audience and trust.** They can be the face or voice of a launch.

Getting good at working with creators is one of the biggest advantages a modern marketer has over the older generation.

## 2. Four ways to use creators

1. **Ads in your ad account.** Real people talking to camera are often the top performers in Meta and TikTok accounts. Use **creative diversity**: many voices, looks, ages and styles (vlogs vs talking to camera), because different people resonate with different customers.
2. **Seeding.** Give product free, ask for nothing, hope they post. Works best when the product costs the business $10–$40. For services: invite local creators to experience it (a free meal, a free detail, a free treatment) with no obligation.
3. **Affiliates.** Creators earn a commission or referral fee on sales they drive: TikTok Shop, ShopMy, LTK, or a simple code ("SARAH10: 10% off, Sarah earns $50 per booking"). Some build whole accounts around affiliate income.
4. **Creating content for or about you:**
   - As the **face of** the brand's own account (a factory's creator posting quests and techniques)
   - On a **separate account** that promotes the brand (whiteboard videos on a topic that promote an app)
   - **Many creators at once** making similar content on their own accounts (one app has dozens of creators of all backgrounds promoting books and habits)

**Pro teams and ambassadors** are the original influencers: sponsored athletes or experts the brand works with every launch. Have 5–6 recurring people. On a small budget, micro creators often get outsized results if you build a good story together.

## 3. Finding creators

**Start with your own customers.** Tools like Outer Signal scan a customer or email list and show which customers are creators or influencers. The easiest win. Manually: who tags you, who leaves great reviews, who already posts about you.

**Platforms**
- **Tribe**: creators who make ads for your account
- **Sideshift**: creators to be on your account or represent you
- **Later**: influencer platform with managed services
- **Meta's creator search** inside its business tools; **Social Snowball** for finding and outreach
- **Agencies** that run creator campaigns
- **Australian-friendly marketplaces:** Collabstr (filter by Melbourne, post a campaign and creators apply with prices), Influee and JoinBrands (UGC videos at set prices), The Right Fit (Australian), Fiverr/Upwork ("UGC creator Australia")

**Manual local search:** Instagram and TikTok searches ("Melbourne home reno", "Melbourne runner", "eastern suburbs mum"), location tags, local hashtags, local Facebook groups. Check: real comments, regular posting, past brand work, and (for influencers) audience location; ask for screenshots.

**It's a numbers game.** Reach out to many; many won't reply. Keep searching and engaging in the niche to learn it.

**AI agent opportunity.** Searching platforms for mentions of a keyword, making a list, vetting whether accounts are active, and sending first outreach is a repetitive task that suits an AI agent.

## 4. The pyramid

```
        ▲  A few anchor influencers: big reach, make it an event, excite other creators
       ▲▲  Mid-tier creators (30k–100k): trusted in a niche, the core
      ▲▲▲  Many micro/new creators: volume of content to test in ads
```
Small business version: 3–5 local micro creators, plus perhaps one mid-tier local name.

## 5. The modular brief

A brief is the instruction sheet for a creator, designer or videographer. The **top 10%** of creators don't need one; give them the basics and let them create. The other **90% need it**, or content is weak and off-message. A modular brief is a long checklist; include only the parts you need (★ = easiest to drop).

1. **Two key messages.** Only two, not ten.
2. **Target audience.** A short persona ("homeowners 35–55 in Melbourne's east, busy parents").
3. **Content type** and how much freedom they have (vlog, talking to camera, static, POV).
4. **Hook inspiration** in three parts: on-screen text, visual, spoken line. Share hooks that already work; reuse winning hooks across creators. Object hooks ("start by unboxing it") work well.
5. **References.** Links to high-performing content you want them to learn from.
6. ★ **Mechanism.** The format: blitz ("10 things…"), storytime, versus, etc.
7. ★ **Simple outline.** Hook → scenario → point → joke → CTA. New creators shouldn't wing it.
8. ★ **Assets.** B-roll, photos, graphics, logos, before/afters; essential for green-screen videos.
9. **Versions needed.** 2–3 hooks; one ending with an organic CTA ("comment RENO"), one with an ad CTA ("book a free quote, link below").
10. **Theme.** If it's part of a campaign, the concept their content should attach to.
11. **Disclosure and rules.** How to label it; claims they must not make (see section 9).

## 6. Seeding systems and long-term partnerships

**Two programs:**
1. **Seeding**: free product, no obligation. Still very effective, especially for cheaper products. Build a system: find useful and up-and-coming people → contact them in a friendly way → send the product → check whether they posted → **graduate** the best into paid work.
2. **Paid**: people paid to promote who generate sales and interest worth their fee.

**Prefer long-term partnerships** (multiple posts over months) over one-off posts.

**Track it in a CRM** (or a simple sheet): who, contact, address, what was sent, when, posted (Y/N), link, performance, next step. Keep it in-house where possible; agencies add a margin and you lose the relationships. A marketer with a 2,000-person seeding list that reliably opens packages is very hireable.

**PR kits.** Design the unboxing (packaging, note, extras). For software: give free access or trials to the top 40 people in the niche.

**Give creators the concept.** A toy brand briefed many creators on a "video-game scenario" theme for a retail-exclusive two-pack; a creator with about 20,000 followers produced a video with hundreds of thousands of likes and millions of YouTube views.

## 7. Whitelisting and darklisting

- **Whitelisting (Meta "partnership ads", TikTok "Spark Ads")**: the creator posts on their own account and gives you an **ad code**; you paste it into Ads Manager ("use partnership ad code") and run it from their account with your budget and targeting. People trust a person more than a brand, so these often outperform. Many strong accounts run 30–50% of ads this way.
- **Darklisting**: the ad runs from the creator's account but was never posted on their feed. Less common, used by more advanced advertisers.
- Advanced teams use a creative "brain" (e.g. Motion's tools) to track which creators, briefs and whitelisted ads perform.

## 8. Agreements

Agree in writing before they start:
- Deliverables (posts, raw files, number of hooks, versions)
- **Raw files** (no text or music) so you can edit and reuse
- **Ad code** for their post
- **Usage rights**: where you can use the content (ads, website, email) and for how long (e.g. paid ads for 3 months)
- Edit permission
- Price (creators usually charge extra for ad usage)
- Disclosure and claims rules
- Never run a creator's video as an ad without written permission.

## 9. Rules in Australia

- Paid or gifted content must be clearly disclosed ("Paid partnership" label, #ad). The AANA Code and the ACCC treat undisclosed ads as misleading.
- Registered health practitioners (AHPRA): no testimonials about clinical outcomes in advertising, including influencer content; avoid "this physio fixed my back" content. Focus on education, experience and the team.
- Health products may fall under the TGA advertising code (e.g. supplements, therapeutic claims).
- Creators must not make claims the business can't prove.
