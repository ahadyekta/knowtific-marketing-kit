## 1. What a campaign is

A campaign focuses all your marketing on **one idea at one moment**, so the effect is bigger than separate posts: a launch (product, service, feature), company news, or a seasonal moment (summer, Black Friday, EOFY). When your posts, your partners' posts, paid creators and retailers all talk about the same thing at the same time, the result compounds: 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 isn't 4, it's more like 6.5, because of how algorithms, attention and repeated exposure work together.

## 2. Strategy: insight or concept

Every campaign starts with one of these, ideally both:
- **Insight**: something true you've discovered about customers: a problem, habit or frustration you can build on. Examples: "everyone knows they need greens, but the experience of taking them is awful, and kids won't do it"; "people treat themselves by buying backups of their favourite lipstick or sunscreen".
- **Concept**: a creative idea or visual world (a "menu" theme for a makeup launch, a mad-scientist lab for a running shoe, "market crash" news headlines for a Black Friday sale on luxury bags positioned as investments).

If the insight is weak, make up for it with a strong concept. Tie the whole campaign to the one point customers must take away, packaged so they're into it.

## 3. Where a campaign runs

| Area | What it means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Core** | Your own marketing: what you already have and can afford | Small business: the Instagram account, the website, the shop sign. Typical business: Instagram, TikTok, founder's social, email and SMS lists, website, SEO, ads |
| **Extensions** | Retailers and partners who support it | Retailer: paid placement, collab posts, newsletter feature, in-store pop-up. Partners: long-term influencer deals, co-promoting companies, integration partners, people in your network |
| **Existing customers** | The most overlooked area. Offer it to them first and involve them in telling the story | Early access before launch, customer pricing, a one-to-one email to every VIP ("want anything before it sells out?"), inviting customers to post about it |
| **Merchandising** | Anything physical that can act as a billboard | Store displays, shelf cards, videos in store, pallet displays in warehouse retailers (design the sides and corners), gondola graphics, the shopfront, vehicles, job site signage |

## 4. The content plan: six phases

Modern launches don't rely on one big video. Plan **15–22 pieces** of organic content per launch, plus ad variations. The loop: post organic first → winners become ads → concepts that work go out to creators and UGC for more ads and reach.

| Phase | Purpose | Pieces | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Tease** | Let fans know something is coming | 1–2 | Low stakes and cheap. If it flops, fine |
| **2. Unveil** | The first time people properly see the thing | 1–3 | Different from the launch: the moment of discovery. Walkthroughs made here become the best assets on the product page and strong ads to warm audiences |
| **3. Launch** | The core "shot on goal" | Several | One hero asset, but take multiple shots: recuts, alternative edits, carousels made from still frames, a meme carousel designed to be shared |
| **4. Extend** | Keep posting after launch day | Ongoing | Tutorials, unboxing, "what's in the box", answers to common questions from the founder or team. Lower views but bottom-of-funnel: helps people buy. Works for B2B software too: launch video → interface walkthrough → screen-share FAQ with personality |
| **5. Performance** | What the ads team needs | Checklist | Problem–solution ad, founder story ad, "selfish desire" ad (what's in it for me), reply-to-a-comment ad, magazine-style editorial, user testimonial, static image, category comparison. See ads.md |
| **6. Sustain & refresh** | Keep it alive | Wave 2 | Traction fades after 2 weeks to 2 months. Plan **wave 2** up front: a second concept or insight, and contract creators for both waves. Answer the top comments and questions from launch |

**Unveil vs launch, an example:** a makeup brand teased with a "let him cook" apron, half-revealed the product wrapped in a napkin ("table's almost ready"), unveiled it with 24-hour early access, then posted the proper launch video and a product showcase, while running the same checklist in its ads account and refreshing retail displays.

## 5. Planning the shoot

- **Outline everything before the shoot.** Without a plan, a shoot day produces two things. With a shot list (teaser single shot, teaser carousel, wide-angle take, drone take, meme carousel stills, normal carousel), one day at one location produces 10+ assets.
- If spending $10k–$500k on a shoot, getting 22 assets beats getting 1–2.
- If your production company can't work this way, they're the wrong firm for the modern era. Good ones are excited by it and don't charge much more.
- **Production matters less than you think.** Low-fi content with a great concept, posted across multiple accounts, often beats polished work. A staffing agency can run a "mad scientist cooked up our new method" concept just as well as a shoe brand.
- **Creator flywheel.** Find an idea that works, then multiply it across 5, 10 or 100 creators, scaling up or down with budget and results.

## 6. The world around the brand

Look at every opportunity around the brand, not just your own channels:

- **Influence.** Sponsored athletes and pro teams were the original influencers. Have 5–6 people you use repeatedly. Small budget? Micro creators can get outsized results if you build a good story together.
- **Retailers.** Being better at social than your retailers is a huge advantage: they want to collab. Trade collab posts and email features. Use your traffic data to get stocked ("we have lots of attention in this city; your stores there should carry us").
- **Partners and resellers.** One surf brand fed hundreds of rental and reseller partners a shared folder of raw and edited content to post. Partners who made great content (about 10% of them) got collab posts, and the brand ran regional paid spend to push customers to them. Give partners the product first and the launch date so they post the next day.
- **Seeding.** Send PR kits for unboxing (design the kit). Software: give free access to the top 40 people in your niche.
- **Email** (your list and paid newsletter placements) and **giveaways**.
- **In person.** Events don't have to be big: invite 40 local customers to the office for pizza; host a VIP Zoom where 100 people from your list get a first look and time with the CEO; launch at a trade show. Large B2B companies run their own conference, launch a big idea there, put it across their channels, bring creators, and use their sponsored media network and ads.
- **Volume.** If results justify it, don't stop at a fixed budget. If $14,000 produced $36,000 in sales, keep going while the economics hold and there's no inventory constraint.
- **Crossover.** Reuse everything: organic as paid, partner posts as ads, influencer posts in emails. The more an asset is reused, the more you can justify making it great.

**Enabled media.** The modern stack is owned (your accounts) + paid (ads) + **enabled** (people you seed, creators, paid influencers, and events or experiences designed so others create content). A billboard's value is partly the photos people take of it. A brand booth at a festival like Coachella (or, closer to home, Splendour in the Grass, the Royal Melbourne Show or a regional field day) is bought for the thousands of influential attendees filming the line and wearing the merch. Then boost or whitelist the best enabled content with ad spend.

**Make concepts easy to join.** The best influencer concepts are simple to take part in ("film yourself dancing in it"), so creators can apply them instantly. Give creators the theme so their content attaches to the launch, e.g. video-game-style scenarios for a toy blaster two-pack.

## 7. Campaign types and the always-on calendar

| Type | Example | Local adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| **Theme campaign** | An activewear brand's "state fair" summer shoot and giveaway, flowing straight into an "out of office, Italy" campaign | A café's "Winter Soup Season" |
| **Anchor launch** | A beauty brand launching its first fragrance with a big influencer as the anchor face | A gym opening a new location with a well-known local trainer leading opening week |
| **Feature bundle** | A software company bundling many updates into a twice-yearly launch event with a microsite, videos, workshops and creators | A clinic announcing new services and online booking together as "the new clinic experience" |
| **Yearly theme** | A gym chain announcing one big message each year, on its walls, social and ads | A builder's yearly "Built to last" theme |

**Go campaign to campaign.** Leading brands move from one concept to the next with little gap: Black Friday → "8 days of Christmas" → New Year → announcing next year's team → Valentine's Day. Running campaigns back to back compounds sales. Even a small brand can chain 4–6 a year.

## 8. Planning grid and asset list

Map each stage against internal and external channels:

| Stage | Internal (social, email/SMS, website, physical location, ads) | External (partners, retailers/resellers, creators and influencers, seeding, PR/podcasts/trade media) |
|---|---|---|
| Tease | | |
| Unveil | | |
| Launch | | |
| Extend / sustain | | |
| Refresh (wave 2) | | |

Then build the **asset list**:
1. **Position**: the one idea for this campaign and why.
2. **Assets**: every piece needed, by phase.
3. **Owner**: who is responsible for each.
4. **Dates and work-back**: deadline for each, worked back from launch day (draft due, review, final, scheduled).
5. Link it to the brand world calendar so every campaign has its place in the year.

## 10. Scaling down for small businesses

You can run the same method at any size. A sole trader might do: 1 teaser story, 1 unveil carousel, a launch video plus 2 recuts, 3 extend videos answering FAQs, an email to past customers with early-access pricing, a post from one partner supplier, and 2 ad variations. Same phases, smaller numbers.
