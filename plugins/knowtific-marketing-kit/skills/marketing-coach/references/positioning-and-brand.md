## 1. What positioning is

Positioning is **what you're known for**: the idea people associate with you, and the lens through which you see the world and the world sees you. In a crowded market it has one job: **be remembered**. When someone learns about you, do they understand what you stand for, why they'd choose you, and do they remember it later?

- **It can't be generic.** "Quality service, friendly team, competitive prices" is what everyone says, so it says nothing.
- **It must be AI-legible.** ChatGPT and Google's AI answers recommend businesses practically: they need a reason to show you. That reason comes from your positioning. A clinic that clearly says "we specialise in running injuries" gets recommended for "best physio for runners in Melbourne"; a generic clinic doesn't.
- **It's relative and a moving target.** Your position depends on what competitors offer, and that changes. A computer with the same specs could be positioned as a gaming PC or a work machine; the angle you choose is the position.
- **It's more than price.** It's who you appeal to and how you're different.
- **It's the north star.** Every ad, post, landing page, email and sales conversation should repeat it. Strong positioning makes ads cheaper, makes people remember you, and makes them refer you more. If a business doesn't have one, finding it is your first job, before any ad spend.

## 2. Tests and exercises

**Shelf test.** Picture the business on a shelf beside every alternative. In energy drinks, each brand has a reason to exist: one is the rebel, one is aimed at women, one feels like a health product, one is sold on its content and culture. For a local business the "shelf" is the Google results page, the Maps pack, or an Instagram feed. Why would someone pick this one?

**Elevator comparison.** Someone says "Oh, like [big competitor]?" You must be able to answer in one or two sentences: "Actually, we do this. Here's the difference, and here's why you'd choose us." If the owner can't answer that crisply, the positioning isn't done.

**Good / better / best.** Big retailers merchandise three tiers: *best* (premium, sold on brand and top-end features), *better* (the core where most sales happen), and *good* (sold mainly on price, trying to feel premium for less). Decide which tier the business is playing in, and price, design and message to match.

## 3. Positioning types

Pick the one or two that are genuinely true for the business. Some need a real product advantage; others can be achieved through marketing alone.

**Needs a real product or service advantage:**

| Type | Idea | Examples | Local adaptation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Performance** | Measurably better than what existed. The strongest position, but most brands don't have it. Prove it with data, tests and vivid metaphors, and repeat it constantly | A supplement with a proprietary absorption method; a protein bar with better macros that compares itself to a piece of fish | "Most hot water systems replaced the same day", if it's true and you can show the numbers |
| **Category creation / new form** | A known thing in a new form factor | A fitness tracker as a ring instead of a watch | Mobile physio that comes to your home; a bathroom renovation sold as a 10-day package |
| **Counter-position** | A better experience than the big existing option for a certain group. Your job is to take share from the worse experience | Greens as gummies instead of bad-tasting powder; Mac vs PC ads; prebiotic soft drink vs regular soft drink | "No call-out fee"; "Book online in 30 seconds" vs ring-and-wait competitors |
| **Smart** | Add technology to a traditionally "dumb" category, which makes the old version feel outdated | Smart mattresses, smart thermostats, smartphones | A plumber who sends a live "tech on the way" tracking link |

**Can be achieved largely through marketing:**

| Type | Idea | Watch out | Local adaptation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Value** | Cheaper, close to as good. Harder for products now because cheap marketplaces dominate on price | Easy to undercut; erodes margin | Still a strong lever for services, local businesses and new agencies winning first clients |
| **Niche** | "For us, by us": one language, background, body type, subculture or identity. Buying becomes an act of support. Ideally the founders are from that group | Limits the total addressable market (TAM) | Farsi-speaking dentist; physio for runners; accountant for tradies |
| **Generational** | Similar product, messaging aimed at one generation | Messaging, imagery and channels must match the age group | Strength training for over-50s |
| **Single attribute (own one word)** | Be synonymous with one word in the customer's mind, then build product and marketing to keep reminding them | You must deliver it every single time | The electrician who owns "on time" (late = free call-out) |
| **Position against** | Pick one bad thing about the popular option and be "not that" | Must be a real, widely felt problem | "Not the builder who disappears for weeks" |
| **Counterculture** | The exact opposite of a category's mainstream look, lifestyle and tone, often pushed to an extreme: darker, louder, more intense. There's room for an extreme player in almost every category | Must be authentic to the owners and customers | A loud, irreverent trades brand in a category of beige vans |
| **Name positioning (oddball)** | Adopt a name that a whole community already loves and identifies with | Can feel gimmicky; check trademarks | A suburb-pride or local-team-flavoured name for a hyperlocal brand |

**Premium positions (charging more):**

| Type | Idea | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **True performance** | Built for people who take the activity seriously | Needs real expertise, background and credibility |
| **Lifestyle performance** | When you serve serious users well, aspirational buyers follow and pay extra to look the part | Most buyers of expert gear don't need it; they aspire to it |
| **Designer** | Aesthetics are the differentiator; people pay for look, care and design thinking | Needs exceptional design and photography |
| **Bespoke** | Truly custom, one of one | Harder to execute; charge a real premium |
| **Luxury without the markup** | A counter-position to luxury: luxury-level quality at a lower price than big luxury houses | A modern spin on value; explain why it's cheaper |
| **Lifestyle premium** | People aspire to the brand and the life it represents; the value is partly intangible | Hard to create on purpose; built over time |

There are hundreds of variations. Once you know the main types, use them as a menu: "Are we selling on value? A more bespoke experience? Higher performance? Better for a certain niche or lifestyle?"

## 4. The positioning statement

Mission statements, vision statements and taglines matter much less than they used to. You need **one clear positioning statement** and **one (or a few) copy styles**. This formula is adapted from Geoffrey Moore's classic framework:

> **For** [target customer] **who** [need or want], **[brand]** is the [category] **that** [main benefit]. **Unlike** [competitors or alternatives], **we** [key difference].

- **Target**: who exactly. Start here.
- **Need**: what they want. It can be practical or emotional: look good, feel safe, save time, make friends, impress someone.
- **Category + benefit**: what you are and what it does for them.
- **Unlike + difference**: what else they might buy instead, and why you're different.

**Examples**
> For distance runners who get side pain, [Brand] is the electrolyte supplement that stops it in seconds. Unlike sugary sports drinks, we give instant relief with no crash.

> For busy homeowners in Melbourne's east who want a new bathroom without months of mess, [Brand] is the renovation company that finishes in 10 working days. Unlike builders who juggle five jobs at once, we do one bathroom at a time at a fixed price.

This single sentence gives any AI tool everything it needs about positioning, audience and offer. Put it at the top of `marketing/brand.md`.

## 5. Copy styles

Choose the styles that suit the brand, and test more than one; many brands test their way into the right mix. Reading the books of the classic writer behind the first two styles will make you better than most marketers at explaining things.

| Style | Feel | Classic writer | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Direct response** | Urgent, confrontational, numbers-driven, names the problem, asks for action with a timeline. Often the highest performing, and the one marketers are most scared of | Dan Kennedy | "Thicker hair in 3–6 months." "Leaking shower? Fixed in one day or it's free. Book before Friday." |
| **Evidence-led** | Calmer, methodical, uses numbers, results and lots of testimonials to tell the story | Claude Hopkins | "400+ bathrooms. 4.9★ from 230 Google reviews. Here's what Sarah from Ringwood said…" |
| **Human / story-driven** | Every piece starts with a person or a moment; casual voice; builds in public | (no classic writer named; think casual brand storytelling, like a burrito brand telling its own story) | "Last week Mark called at 7am. His only bathroom flooded the night before his daughter's wedding…" |
| **Culturally savvy** | Pop-culture references, jokes, trends; shows the brand is tapped in | (modern fashion brands) | Playful, trend-referencing email subject lines. Only works if the brand and customers are culturally fluent |

For most trades and health clients, start by testing direct response against evidence-led. For AHPRA-regulated health clients, keep direct response within the advertising rules (no outcome guarantees, no clinical testimonials).

**Working with AI drafts.** Load the positioning statement, services and real testimonials, ask for versions in each style, then add the human layer: real stories, real numbers, details only the business knows. An AI draft is the start, not the finished copy.

## 6. The taste economy: how people buy now

**What changed**
1. **Supply chains were democratised.** Anyone can find a factory anywhere. First came a flood of cheap copies; then competition made the *nice* version barely more expensive than the basic one.
2. **Caring is easy.** You used to have to dig to learn what's good. Now social media brings education and taste to you: teenagers know fragrance notes, shoppers know skincare ingredients, everyone has seen a nicer apartment than theirs. Algorithms notice what you like and show you more of it.

**The result:** people buy "little luxuries", the designed version that costs a bit more, and use taste as identity (what they like, share and buy says who they are). New brands can feel established fast by building a beautiful world around simple products.

**The educated consumer.** Buyers now research ingredients and materials (a skincare ingredient printed on the front of the pack; cotton vs polyester vs bamboo; gut health). They follow the journey and expect a slightly better thing.

**Three ways to sell to them**
1. **The best.** People still search "best X", "worst X", "X reviews", and they do it on TikTok and Instagram as well as Google. Own a "best" claim ("the softest hoodie") and back it with the story of how you made it the best, told by many creators and ads. Claims must be provable (see australia-compliance.md).
2. **The hero ingredient or material.** "Made with [thing people are learning about]." For services: the hero material or method ("we only use [premium waterproofing system]").
3. **The transformation.** What changes in my life? "It ties the room together", "you'll get your weekends back", "back to running in six weeks".

**Enthusiasm is the by-product of education.** The more people learn, the more excited they get and the more they want to take part. Education content is selling.

## 7. Premium buyer types

Who overpays for premium, and why. Decide which one or two the business serves, then plan the product and marketing roadmap for them (choosing the right archetype changes price, channel and message).

| Type | Who they are | How to sell to them |
|---|---|---|
| **Cultural omnivore** | Educated, experienced, enjoys the best and the humble equally (fine wine and a taco truck). Taste built through real exposure. Won't buy entry-level luxury | Charge a premium only for genuinely better quality or experience. They can tell. A great target if you're worth it |
| **Aspirational overspender** ("petite bourgeoisie") | Starting to have money, reads the newsletters, follows creators' recommendations, overspends to signal they've arrived | Entry-level premium products; show what they get, how it feels, the recognition, and how it starts their journey with the brand |
| **True elite** | Wealth is normal; a $3,000 purchase is just a purchase. Hard to reach with social ads; doesn't follow brands on Instagram | In-person: demo days at clubs, boat shows, private events; long-form storytelling and documentaries shared in their circles |
| **Aspiring** | Not educated in the category; wants the logo and the signal, whether or not they can afford it | Too much of this and you lose your premium image; none and you miss future customers. Balance carefully |

**Everyday (non-premium) clients**, such as most tradies, usually sort more simply into **price-driven** buyers (cheapest acceptable option, shop around) and **quality/convenience-driven** buyers (want it done right, fast and without hassle, will pay more). Decide which one the business is built for; the four types above matter when a business sells a premium tier. (The everyday split is Knowtific's addition.)

True luxury is a generational play: built through craft and experience over a long time, often not at luxury prices at first. Very few brands break into the top tier quickly.

## 8. Brand lore

Lore is the depth and story around a brand that makes people care: the inspiration, process and people behind it. Ways to build it:
- Publish mood boards for a collection or project, behind-the-scenes from shoots, lookbooks, design process.
- Show the craft: materials, methods, the details competitors don't talk about.
- Give recurring characters a voice (founder, lead tradesperson, designer).
- Tell origin stories and "why we do it this way" stories.

For a builder or designer: a project blog with the mood board, the material choices and the reasons, then the finished result. It builds lore and it's useful reference for future clients.

## 9. The brand world map

A planning exercise for a new client, a new role, or a strategy reset. Run it as a **paid workshop**; if the client then signs a retainer, the fee is credited against it. It's also powerful when applying for a marketing job: arrive having mapped their brand world.

Draw four areas and fill each in.

**Characters: who can represent the brand?**
- Founder or owner on camera; experts (designers, lead tradespeople, practitioners)
- Social media team or hired creators
- Customers who make content (tools like Outer Signal find influencers in a customer list)
- Collaborators: partner companies, suppliers, executives who have podcasts, anyone in the network with a voice

**Sets: where can you film?**
- **Evergreen**: office, showroom, clinic, workshop, warehouse ("pack an order with us"), factory, van, a good-looking corner you build, a hired studio with a cyc wall once a month
- **Temporary**: job sites before/during/after, trade show booths (film the night before, when everything is set up and empty), events
- If a team is far away, send them a small gimbal camera (e.g. an Osmo) with a shot list, or hire a local videographer through Upwork

**Events: a calendar by month or quarter**
- **Company**: launches, new services, offsites, the trade show you attend every year, portfolio updates, hackathons, competitions. Software and service businesses: bundle many small feature updates into one release event a couple of times a year
- **Culture**: what customers are thinking about at that time (New Year, Valentine's Day, school holidays, EOFY, footy finals, spring, Black Friday, Christmas). Ask AI: "What do my target customers pay attention to through the year?"
- **Industry**: trade shows, regulation changes, competitor launches. Decide: launch at the same time head-to-head, just before them to steal attention, just after to cut it off, or at a completely different time

**Plot: the storylines**
- **Events** become plots for your characters on your sets
- **Enemies**: how you respond to competitor moves (lead, support, counter-position)
- **Friends**: partners, collaborators and regular creators. Ask what they're planning this year (tours, events, launches) and where you can plug in or piggyback
- **Specials**: things you invent to fill quiet periods: your own event, a social show or episodic series (one luggage brand released a run of episodes in a dull late-summer period to build affinity before the big sales season)

**Find the holes.** No one will go on camera → hire or find someone. Plenty of people but nowhere that looks good → build a set. Nothing in Q3 → create a special. Revisit the map monthly or quarterly as you find new people, sets and friends. The calendar feeds everything else: social plans, campaigns and the weekly email agenda.
