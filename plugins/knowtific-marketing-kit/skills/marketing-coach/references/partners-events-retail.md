## 1. Events

**Three kinds of event:**

| Type | What it is | How to make it pay |
|---|---|---|
| **Selling event** (consumer shows, markets, conventions, car shows) | Your customers come to buy | Aim to at least break even on direct sales while gaining awareness. Build a repeatable format, and bring an extroverted salesperson: selling in person still works |
| **B2B trade show** | Retail buyers or business customers come to find suppliers | Costs more. You must drive it: book meetings with buyers beforehand (LinkedIn, cold email), pull people to the booth, follow up fast. Know your distribution and capacity. Set a goal: "this cost $X, so we need $Y in orders (POs) or direct sales" |
| **Customer appreciation event** | Thank fans, create an experience | Hard to justify on ROI unless you make it social-media friendly and use it to create content. If it takes off online, it's worth more than ever |

**Piggyback before you host.** Launch at an existing trade show or industry event; plug into a partner's event; or host your own when you're big enough (some software companies run a conference and launch their big idea there).

**Small events count:** invite 40 local customers to the office for pizza; a VIP Zoom with your top customers getting a first look and time with the founder; a workshop in the showroom. Real fans often enjoy a candid, casual event more than a polished one.

**Design events for content (enabled media).** A brand booth at a big festival like Coachella (in Australia: Splendour in the Grass, a royal show, a home show or a field day) is bought for the thousands of influential attendees filming the line and wearing the merch. Ask of every event: what will people film and share?

**Film the set.** The night before a trade show, when the booth is built and empty, is a great time to shoot content.

## 2. Partnerships and collaborations

Partnerships are 1 + 1 = 2.5: working with others pushes your brand further.

**Collaborations** (two brands co-create a product or offer and co-market it). What usually decides success is logistics: who pays for production, how revenue is split, who sells it, who makes which assets, timelines. Break down responsibilities and agree them in writing.
- **Collaboration calendar:** look at your normal release schedule (spring, autumn, one new product a year) and fill the gaps with collabs.
- **Collaboration programs:** some big brands run an ongoing series where different partners fill slots (Moncler's Genius program is the well-known example). Good for a bigger brand working with smaller ones.
- **Local service versions:** a builder + interior designer package; a physio + running shop event; a café + local roaster limited blend; a plumber + bathroom showroom offer.

**Co-promotion partners:** companies whose customers overlap with yours (integration partners in software, suppliers, complementary trades). Ask what they're planning this year and where you can plug in.

## 3. Licensing

Pay a royalty to use popular intellectual property (sports teams, characters, artists) on your product. A fashion brand that licenses sports leagues sells versions for every team to fans who want to support both the brand and their team. Consider it once you have the volume to support the fees.

## 4. Retail support

Getting into stores means answering "what's in it for me?", and once you're in, the product must sell.

**Getting stocked: why should they stock you?**
- Bring data: "we have lots of search and social traffic in your region, and nowhere to buy us there"
- Bring marketing: "we'll dedicate creators to support your chain specifically"
- Fill a gap: "your shelf only has expensive options; customers want a cheaper alternative"
- Being better at social media than your retailers makes them want to collab with you: trade collab posts, get featured in their emails and newsletters, then push your views and traffic to them

**Merchandising (once you're in):** how you're presented in store.
- Entry level: shelf-ready tear-back boxes with branding
- **Aisle interrupters**: tags sticking out into the aisle calling attention to the product
- Displays, gondola graphics, end caps, pallet displays (design the corners and sides to show your value)
- Swap displays for a campaign
- Retail psychology: if someone stops and looks at your product, you have a high chance of the sale. Your job is to win that first look; then the product has to lose it.

**Retail staff training.** Stores with trained floor staff (outdoor, sporting goods, electronics) sell more of what staff know and like. Use online training, in-person sessions and **field teams**, so every salesperson has used the product, would recommend it, and can answer the key questions. Incentives help.

## 5. Partner and reseller networks

If your product is sold or used by many partners (resellers, rental operators, studios, gyms, installers):
- Give partners the product and the launch date early so they can post the next day.
- Keep a shared folder of raw and edited content they can post or re-edit.
- About 10% of partners will make great content; collab-post with them and use their content in your ads.
- Run regional paid spend to send customers to partners, and core paid spend to the product.
- The result is a world of content: your athletes and experts, your retail partners, your rental partners and your core brand all posting together.
- Local service version: a supplier's installer network, a franchise network, or partner trades sharing job content.
